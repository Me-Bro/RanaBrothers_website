> **Short answer:** Choose on evidence you can check, not on the pitch: working products you can use, engineers you can meet, a written scope and process, a signed intellectual property (IP) assignment, repository access from day one, and an itemised price. Score each company on the 20 checks below, and treat missing ownership terms as a blocker.

Knowing how to choose a software development company is mostly knowing which evidence to ask for. Polished case studies are easy to produce. The evidence that counts is the kind you can check yourself: a product you can use, engineers you can meet, a contract you can read before you pay. This guide turns that into 20 checks, a list of first-call questions, and notes on freelancers, agencies and contracts.

## How to choose a software development company: six areas to check

| Area | What you are testing | Evidence to ask for |
|---|---|---|
| Proof of work | Has the company shipped something like yours, and does it still work? | Live products, case studies with decisions, a code walkthrough, two references |
| Who builds it | Will the people you meet be the people who build? | Named engineers, seniority mix, a subcontracting policy |
| Process | Can they run a project in the open? | A written scope, demos of working software, tests, a release routine |
| Contracts & IP | Will you own what you pay for? | A signed IP assignment, repository and account access, exit terms |
| Pricing | Is the price explained, or only asserted? | An itemised estimate with assumptions, payments tied to milestones |
| Red flags | What should make you slow down? | Vague answers, pressure, dependence on the vendor |

## Where do I find companies worth shortlisting?

Start with three or four. Good sources, roughly in order of reliability: founders you know who shipped something similar, case studies with live links you can open, independent review sites, and public engineering work such as articles or open-source repositories. Treat review sites as a starting signal. Companies can invite favourable reviews, so read the critical ones and verify claims yourself.

Shortlist on fit as well as quality: experience with your kind of product, working hours that overlap with yours, and a project size that matters to the company. Then send each company the same one-page brief. The questions they send back tell you more than the quote does, and identical inputs make the quotes comparable.

## The 20-point checklist

Copy the list into a spreadsheet, one column per company. Score every point: 0 if the company fails it or has no answer, 1 if it passes on its own word, 2 if you have seen the evidence. Compare totals, but treat a 0 on any point under Contracts & IP as a blocker, whatever the total.

### Proof of work

How do I verify a developer's portfolio? Treat it as a claim and test it four ways.

1. **Use their live products.** Open two or three products they built and run the main flow in the browser or from the app store. Check that it works, loads reasonably and has been updated recently. Screenshots and design mock-ups say nothing about engineering.
2. **Ask what exactly they built.** For each case study, ask which parts this company wrote, what the client's team did and which decision they would now change. Real answers contain specifics and trade-offs.
3. **Ask for a code or architecture walkthrough.** A screen-share with an engineer is enough: repository layout, tests, how a change is released, how decisions are documented. Work under an NDA can be shown with details removed. If you are not technical, bring an engineer you trust.
4. **Call two references yourself.** Choose projects of a similar size or type, and contact the references directly. Ask what went wrong, how the company handled it and whether they would hire it again.

### Who builds it

5. **Meet the engineers before you sign.** Ask for a call with the people who will write the code, not only the salesperson or account manager. Note whether they ask about your users and constraints or only about your budget.
6. **Get names, roles and allocation.** Ask who is on the team, the seniority mix, and what share of each person's time your project gets. "A team of experts" is not an answer.
7. **Get the subcontracting policy in writing.** Ask whether any work goes to other firms or freelancers, who they are, and whether your confidentiality and IP terms bind them too.

### Process

8. **Ask how an idea becomes a plan.** Look for a discovery step, or a written scope with assumptions and exclusions, before any fixed price. A quote with no discovery is a guess.
9. **See working software every sprint.** The [Agile Manifesto](https://agilemanifesto.org/principles.html) calls working software the primary measure of progress. Ask for a demo of a staging build you can open yourself, not a slide deck. Agree the rhythm too: overlap hours, a tracker you can read, and one named contact.
10. **Ask how quality is protected.** Look for code review, automated tests, a staging environment, a release routine they can describe step by step, and a clear process for fixing defects after launch. Ask how they handle secrets, backups and dependency updates.

### Contracts & IP

11. **Ownership is assigned to you in writing.** Source code, designs and documentation pass to you in a signed document, with the moment of transfer stated. Do not assume that paying is enough; the contract notes below explain why.
12. **Confidentiality, open source and AI tools are covered.** Expect an NDA before you share data or the idea, a list of open-source and third-party components with their licences, and a stated policy on AI coding tools: what is sent to them and how their output is reviewed.
13. **You hold the keys from day one.** The repository sits in your organisation. Cloud, domain, analytics and app-store developer accounts are opened in your name, with the vendor added as a user. Never the other way round.
14. **Change, exit and handover are defined.** The contract says how scope changes are priced and approved, how either side can end it, and what you receive on exit: code, documentation, deployment notes, credentials and a handover session. Rehearse the handover before the final payment: someone outside the vendor should be able to build and deploy from the documentation alone.

### Pricing

15. **The pricing model fits the uncertainty.** Fixed price suits a well-defined scope. Time and materials, ideally with a cap or a monthly budget, suits exploratory work. A common hybrid is a fixed-price discovery followed by capped sprints. Ask why they recommend their model for your project.
16. **The estimate is itemised.** You should see what is in, what is out, the assumptions behind it, and which third-party costs (cloud, licences, store fees, messaging, AI usage) are separate and recurring. Ask whether the figure includes applicable taxes. A low quote may simply leave out what the others include, so compare line by line.
17. **Payments follow accepted milestones.** Tie payments to delivered and accepted work. If an advance is requested, ask what it covers and what happens if either side stops.

### Red flags

What are the red flags when outsourcing? Three patterns matter most.

18. **A fixed price and date before any scoping.** "Everything in four weeks" for an undefined scope, or a quote that arrives without a single question, means the number is a sales tool.
19. **Pressure, or reluctance to show their work.** Look for discounts that expire this week, evasion about who builds, and no references or demo build.
20. **Anything that makes you dependent on them.** Code held only on the vendor's servers, accounts in the vendor's name, no documentation, or "we will sort out access later".

## Questions to ask on the first call

What questions should I ask an app development company? These twelve cover the checklist. The right-hand column describes what a useful answer sounds like.

| Question | What it tests | A good answer sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| Which of your products can I use today? | Proof of work | Live links, and which parts they built. |
| Who will write the code, and can I speak to them? | Who builds it | Names and roles, and a call before you sign. |
| Do you subcontract any work? | Hidden dependencies | A plain yes or no. If yes, to whom, and under the same terms. |
| How do you get from an idea to an estimate? | Process | A discovery step, or a written scope with assumptions. |
| What will I see at the end of each sprint? | Visibility | A demo of working software on a staging build. |
| Who owns the code, and when does ownership pass? | IP | A signed assignment, with the moment of transfer stated. |
| Whose name will the repository and the cloud, domain and store accounts carry? | Control | Yours, from day one. |
| What happens when scope changes or a milestone slips? | Change control | A written process covering approval and price impact. |
| What does the quote leave out? | Pricing honesty | A short list of exclusions and recurring third-party costs. |
| What do I get if we stop halfway? | Exit | The code, documentation and a handover session for the work done. |
| Who supports the product after launch, and on what terms? | Continuity | Defined support and defect handling, plus knowledge transfer to your team. |
| Tell me about a project that went wrong. | Candour | A specific story, and what changed afterwards. |

## Should I hire a freelancer or an agency?

No option is safer by type. The right choice depends on how much risk the project carries and how much of it you can manage yourself.

| Option | Strongest when | Main risk | Reduce the risk by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Freelancer | The scope is small and well defined, or you need one skill added to a team you already have. | One person is a single point of failure: illness, other clients, no second reviewer. | Keeping the repository in your organisation, adding a reviewer and requiring handover notes. |
| Studio | You want design, build and release from one focused team, with direct access to the people doing the work. | Capacity. A team is finite, so one large project can crowd out others. | Asking who is allocated to you, and for how long. |
| Large agency | There are many workstreams, or formal procurement and compliance needs. | Layers between you and the engineers. Your project may be small to them. | Naming the delivery team in the contract. |
| In-house hire | The product is your core business and you expect to build it for years. | Recruiting takes time, and you carry the management load. | Starting with a studio or freelancer while you recruit, with the repository in your organisation. |

The 20 checks apply to any outside team. With a freelancer, weigh the single-point-of-failure risk heavily. With an agency, weigh who actually builds. We run a studio, so we are not neutral on the studio row.

## What should the contract include?

A contract does not replace judgement, but it is where disputes end up. Take this outline to a lawyer. It is general information, not legal advice.

- Parties, scope and deliverables, with acceptance criteria for each.
- A payment schedule tied to milestones.
- Assignment of IP, as described below, plus a licence to anything the vendor reuses from earlier projects.
- Confidentiality and data-protection obligations.
- Disclosure of open-source and third-party components.
- A change-control process.
- A warranty period in which defects are fixed.
- Support terms after launch.
- Termination rights and handover on exit.
- Liability limits, governing law and dispute resolution.

Ownership deserves care. Under Section 17 of the [Copyright Act, 1957](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/handle/123456789/1367), the author of a work is its first owner, with exceptions such as work made in the course of employment under a contract of service. Computer programmes count as literary works under Section 2(o). So paying a company for software does not by itself make you the owner. Section 19 says an assignment is valid only if it is in writing and signed by the assignor. It must identify the work and specify the rights assigned, the duration and the territory, and the Act fills gaps: if no period is stated, the assignment is deemed to last five years, and if no territory is stated, it is presumed to cover India only. An assignment can also lapse if the rights are not exercised within a year, unless the contract says otherwise. A good contract therefore names the rights, the duration, the territory and the moment ownership passes, and deals with that lapse.

Two practical points follow. Many contracts transfer ownership on final payment, so if you might stop midway, ask for transfer milestone by milestone. And ask what the vendor keeps: libraries and templates it owned before your project usually stay with it, so you need a licence to keep using them. If you or the vendor are outside India, other law may apply.
